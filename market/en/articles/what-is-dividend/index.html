What Is a Dividend and How Does It Work

A beginner's guide to what a dividend is, why companies pay one, which dates decide who receives it, and why an unusually high yield is not always good news.

What Is a Dividend?

A profitable company has several options: reinvest the money in the business, pay down debt, keep it in reserve — or hand part of it to shareholders. That part is called a dividend. A shareholder is a small part-owner of the company, and the dividend is how some of the profits reach them directly, usually as cash paid into the account holding the shares.

A dividend is set per share, so holding more shares means receiving proportionally more. Some companies pay every quarter, some once a year, and some only occasionally, when circumstances allow.

Why Some Companies Pay and Others Don't

Paying a dividend is a decision by the company's management and board, not an obligation. Established companies with steady profits tend to pay, because they cannot always use all the cash they accumulate. Younger, growing companies often prefer to keep the money and spend it on development, hiring or expansion.

So avoid quick conclusions: a company that pays no dividend is not necessarily weak, and a company that pays one is not necessarily strong. The dividend is one data point in a much wider picture.

The Dates Worth Knowing

Every payout comes with a few dates worth understanding:

  • Declaration date — the day the company announces a dividend, how much per share, and when.
  • Ex-dividend date — the date that decides in practice who is entitled. Whoever bought the share before this date receives the dividend; whoever buys on it or later does not receive this payout.
  • Payment date — the day the money is actually paid to entitled shareholders.

Something else usually happens on the ex-dividend date: the share price is adjusted downward by roughly the amount of the dividend. The logic is simple — the cash is about to leave the company, so the company is worth slightly less. In practice the price keeps moving for other reasons too, so the adjustment is not always easy to spot.

A dividend is not money created out of thin air: it moves value from the company's accounts to its shareholders' pockets, and it is never guaranteed.

Dividend Yield — and When a High Number Is a Warning Sign

Dividend yield is the ratio between the annual dividend per share and the share price. As an illustration only: a share priced at 100 that pays out 4 a year trades at a dividend yield of 4%.

There is a trap hiding here. The yield also rises when the price falls: if that same share drops to 50 and its most recent dividend was 4, the yield suddenly reads 8%. The number looks tempting, but it may reflect market concern that the company is in trouble and the dividend will soon be cut. An unusually high yield is a reason to ask questions, not a reason to get excited.

Reinvesting Dividends

Some people use dividends as regular income, while others choose to buy additional shares with them. In the second case, the new shares may earn dividends of their own in the future — creating a compounding effect over the years. This effect depends on the company continuing to pay and on the shares holding their value, and neither is certain.

What to Keep in Mind

A dividend is not a commitment. A company can reduce it, suspend it or cancel it altogether, sometimes at short notice, especially in difficult periods. A share that pays a generous dividend can still lose value, and the fall in price is sometimes far larger than the amount received.

Dividends are also usually taxed, and the tax is often withheld before the money reaches the account, so the amount received is lower than the amount announced. Tax rules differ between countries and between account types, and they are worth understanding. The bottom line: a dividend is one piece of the picture, not enough on its own to judge a company.
