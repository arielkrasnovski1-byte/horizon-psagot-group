Common Crypto Scams and How to Spot Them

The patterns behind most crypto scams, the red flags worth knowing, the habits that protect you, and what to actually do if you have already been hit.

Why Crypto in Particular?

A blockchain transfer is usually irreversible: there is no central body you can call and ask to cancel it. The field is fairly new, full of technical terms, and surrounded by stories of fast profits. That combination suits fraudsters well. The good news: most scams repeat a handful of patterns, and people who know them spot them sooner.

The Common Patterns

  • Guaranteed-return "investment" schemes (Ponzi) — a fixed profit is promised, and earlier members are paid with newcomers' money until the flow stops and everything collapses.
  • Fake exchanges and apps — a professional-looking site shows "profits" on screen, but withdrawing turns out to be impossible.
  • Phishing — a message or website imitating a familiar service in order to extract a password or your wallet's seed phrase.
  • Impersonation — of a celebrity "recommending" an investment, or of a support agent who contacts you first and offers help.
  • Romance scams — a relationship built patiently over weeks, and only then an "investment opportunity". The method is sometimes called "pig butchering".
  • Pump-and-dump groups — the organizers inflate the price of a small coin and sell it to the group's members just before it falls.
  • Fake giveaways — "send coins and get double back". What you send does not return.
  • Rug pulls — founders raise money for a new project or coin, then disappear with it.

Red Flags

The story changes from scam to scam, but the signs repeat. Stop when you meet any of them:

  • A promise of guaranteed, fixed or "risk-free" profit.
  • Urgency: "the offer closes today", "only a few places left".
  • A request for your seed phrase, or to install remote-access software on your computer or phone.
  • Pressure to recruit friends and family.
  • An unlicensed entity, or one where you cannot find out who is behind it.
  • A demand to pay a "tax" or "release fee" before you can withdraw your own money.
No legitimate party will ever ask for your wallet's seed phrase, and no one can guarantee a profit. Anyone who does either has given you the signal to stop.

Habits That Protect You

Protection is built from simple habits. Keep your seed phrase on paper, in a safe place, and never photograph it or type it into any website. Turn on two-factor authentication on every account. Reach websites through an address you typed yourself or a saved bookmark, not through a link that arrived in a message. Before depositing money, check with the supervising authority whether the entity holds a license. And take your time: talk to someone close who is not involved, because a genuine offer will still be there tomorrow. A small successful withdrawal proves nothing either; sometimes it is part of the bait.

What to Do If You Were Hit

First, stop sending money, even if you are promised that "one more payment" will release everything. Then document: screenshots of the conversations, wallet addresses, transaction IDs, phone numbers and website addresses. Report quickly to the police, and to your bank, card company or the platform the money passed through; sometimes an incomplete transfer can still be stopped. Change your passwords, and if your seed phrase was exposed, move whatever is left to a new wallet.

Beware of "fund recovery experts" who approach you after the scam and promise to get the money back for an upfront fee. In most cases this is a second round of the same fraud.

What to Keep in Mind

The chance of recovering money sent to fraudsters is low, so the real protection comes before the transfer, not after it. Methods keep evolving, and no list covers them all. Careful, experienced people fall for scams too, and shame is what delays reporting; there is no need for it. Remember too that a perfectly genuine crypto asset is still volatile and can cause losses: spotting scams reduces one risk, not all of them.
