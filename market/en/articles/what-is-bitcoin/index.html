What Is Bitcoin and How Does It Work

A plain-language introduction to Bitcoin, the technology behind it, and why it behaves so differently from money in a bank.

What Bitcoin Actually Is

Bitcoin is a form of digital money that exists entirely on the internet. Unlike the money in your bank account, no single company, government, or central bank issues it or controls it. Instead, Bitcoin runs on a global network of computers that all follow the same set of rules. When these rules were released in 2009, they defined a fixed system that no single party can quietly change.

People often describe Bitcoin as "digital gold" because there will only ever be a limited supply. The rules cap the total number of coins that can exist, and new coins enter circulation slowly and predictably. This scarcity is one reason many people find it interesting, though it is also part of why its price can move sharply.

The Blockchain in Simple Terms

At the heart of Bitcoin is a technology called the blockchain. Imagine a shared public notebook that records every transaction ever made. Copies of this notebook are stored on thousands of computers around the world at the same time. When someone sends Bitcoin, the transaction is broadcast to the network and eventually written into this shared record.

Because so many independent copies exist, there is no single version that a bad actor can secretly edit. To change one entry, you would have to alter it on a majority of computers simultaneously — a task that is practically impossible on a large network. This is what people mean when they call Bitcoin "decentralized" and "trustless": you do not have to trust any one institution.

Bitcoin replaces trust in a central authority with trust in open rules and mathematics that anyone can verify.

How New Bitcoin Is Created

New transactions are grouped into "blocks" and added to the chain through a process called mining. Miners are specialized computers that compete to solve a difficult mathematical puzzle. The first to solve it gets to add the next block and receives newly created Bitcoin as a reward. This is how fresh coins enter circulation and how the network stays secure.

  • Mining requires large amounts of computing power and electricity.
  • The reward for mining shrinks over time in scheduled steps.
  • This gradual slowdown reinforces Bitcoin's limited supply.

Over time, mining rewards decrease, which means the pace of new coins entering the market keeps falling until the maximum supply is eventually reached.

How People Hold and Use It

To own Bitcoin, you use a digital wallet. A wallet does not literally store coins; it stores the secret keys that prove ownership and let you approve transactions. Whoever controls the keys controls the funds, which is why security is so important. Losing your keys can mean losing access to your Bitcoin permanently.

People use Bitcoin in different ways: some treat it as a long-term store of value, others trade it, and some use it to send money across borders. Each approach carries its own considerations, and none guarantees a profit.

Understanding the Risks

Bitcoin is known for large and rapid price swings. Its value can rise or fall significantly in a short time, driven by news, sentiment, and global demand. It is not backed by a government or a physical asset, and regulations continue to evolve around the world.

None of this is investment advice. Bitcoin can be a fascinating subject to learn about, but anyone considering it should understand the technology, only ever risk what they can afford to lose, and take the time to research thoroughly before making any decision.
