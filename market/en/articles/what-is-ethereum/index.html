What Is Ethereum and How Is It Different From Bitcoin

A beginner's guide to the Ethereum network and its currency, ether: smart contracts, gas fees and proof of stake — and the main differences from Bitcoin.

A Network and a Currency — Two Different Things

Many people say "Ethereum" when they mean a digital currency, but these are really two separate things. Ethereum is a decentralised network: many computers around the world that together maintain a shared blockchain, with no single body in control. Ether is the network's currency — you pay with it to use the network, and it is also the asset traded on the market.

The distinction explains what Ethereum was created for. Bitcoin was built mainly to transfer and store value. Ethereum was built as general-purpose infrastructure — a kind of shared world computer on which anyone can run programs.

Smart Contracts

These programs are called smart contracts. A smart contract is a piece of code stored on the blockchain that runs automatically when its conditions are met. Think of a vending machine: you insert a coin, choose a product, and the machine delivers it — with no shopkeeper in the middle.

Once a smart contract is on the network it is very hard to change, and it does exactly what it was written to do. That is its strength, but also its weak point: a mistake in the code will be carried out just as precisely.

Decentralised Apps and Gas Fees

Smart contracts are the building blocks of decentralised applications — services that run not on one company's servers but on the network itself. Examples include financial services without an intermediary, stablecoins pegged to an ordinary currency, and unique digital assets.

Every action on the network — sending ether or running a smart contract — requires computing work, paid for with a fee known as gas. The fee is not fixed: it depends on how complex the action is and how busy the network is. At busy times fees can spike, sometimes becoming high relative to the amount sent. The fee is also charged even when the action fails.

Proof of Stake in Simple Words

Every blockchain needs a mechanism that decides who adds the next block and ensures they do so honestly. In Bitcoin that is the job of miners, who compete using computing power. Ethereum uses a different method, called proof of stake.

Here, participants called validators lock up ether as a deposit. The network selects who among them proposes the next block and who confirms it, and rewards them. A validator who tries to cheat, or fails to do the job, can lose part of the deposit. Security thus rests on money at stake rather than on electricity — which is why this method uses far less energy.

How Ethereum Differs From Bitcoin

  • Purpose — Bitcoin is meant mainly for transferring and storing value; Ethereum is a platform for running programs and applications.
  • Supply policy — Bitcoin has a supply cap fixed in its code. Ether has none: new coins are issued as rewards to validators, and part of the fees is burned and leaves circulation.
  • Technology — Bitcoin is secured by mining, Ethereum by proof of stake.
  • Pace of change — Bitcoin changes slowly and cautiously; Ethereum is upgraded more often, with the benefits and risks that brings.
Bitcoin is first and foremost digital money; Ethereum is infrastructure to build on — and ether is the fuel that keeps it running.

Risks Worth Knowing

Ether is highly volatile; its price can rise or fall sharply in a short time. Ethereum also has risks of its own: bugs in smart contracts have led to lost funds in the past, and the network's complexity makes it hard for ordinary users to understand exactly what they are approving. The field also attracts many scams and dubious projects.

As with any digital asset, a completed transaction cannot be reversed, and losing your private key means losing access to the asset. Regulation is still taking shape and may change. There is no promise of returns, and you can lose some or all of your money — so learn in depth before making any decision.
