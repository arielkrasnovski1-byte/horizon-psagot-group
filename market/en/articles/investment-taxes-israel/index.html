Tax on Stocks and Crypto in Israel — The Principles

A general overview of how investments are taxed in Israel: when a tax event arises, who withholds and who reports, how crypto is treated, and why professional advice matters.

What Is Actually Taxed?

In Israel, the basic principle is that investments are taxed on profit, not on merely holding an asset. As long as an asset — a share, a fund or a digital coin — stays in your hands while its price rises, the gain exists only on paper. The tax event generally arises at realisation, meaning when the asset is sold.

The tax is generally calculated on the real gain: the difference between the sale price and the purchase price, adjusted for inflation under the applicable rules. The aim is to tax actual profit, not a rise that merely reflects money losing value.

Tax Rate, Dividends and Losses

As a general rule, the tax rate on a real capital gain for individuals in Israel is currently 25%. There are exceptions — for example, different rules for a "substantial shareholder" in a company, and a surtax on high incomes.

A dividend — a share of a company's profits distributed to its shareholders — is also taxed, even though you sold nothing. In many cases the tax is deducted before the money ever reaches your account.

And what about losses? In general terms, a realised capital loss can be offset against capital gains, so the tax reflects the overall result rather than each profitable trade alone. The precise rules — what offsets what, and when a loss can be carried forward to later years — are detailed and worth checking with a professional.

Who Withholds the Tax and Who Reports?

Here the investment routes diverge in practice:

  • Israeli banks and investment houses — usually withhold the tax at source: it is calculated and passed to the Tax Authority at sale, and you receive the net amount.
  • Foreign brokers — generally do not withhold Israeli tax. Reporting the gains and paying the tax is typically the investor's responsibility.
  • Crypto — here too, usually nobody withholds the tax for you, and reporting and payment are your responsibility.

In other words, no withholding does not mean no tax is owed. Anyone using a route without withholding at source should find out in advance which reporting obligations apply.

Tax on investments is generally paid on a realised real gain — and when nobody withholds it at source, the responsibility to report and pay shifts to you.

Crypto: An Asset, Not a Currency

The Israel Tax Authority's position is that digital coins are an asset rather than a currency. Selling them at a profit is therefore generally subject to capital gains tax, as with other assets.

One point surprises many: under the same approach, exchanging one digital coin for another, or paying for a product with it, is usually treated as a sale — a tax event — even if not a single shekel reached your bank account. Someone whose activity is extensive and frequent may be classified differently — one more reason to get individual clarification.

Why Keeping Records Matters

Calculating a gain or loss takes data, and some of it is hard to reconstruct later. It is common to keep, on an ongoing basis:

  • Purchase and sale dates, quantities and prices.
  • Fees paid on each transaction.
  • Periodic statements and confirmations from the institution through which you traded.
  • For crypto — a log of coin-to-coin exchanges and of transfers between wallets as well.

Orderly records matter most when your activity is spread across several institutions, or when nobody consolidates the data for you.

What to Keep in Mind

This article presents general principles only and is not tax advice. Tax laws, rates and the Tax Authority's positions change over time, and every case is different: other income, family status, tax residency, the type of asset and the scale of activity can all change the picture.

A mistake or a failure to report can be costly, including surcharges and penalties. Before relying on any rule, and certainly before filing a report, it is wise to consult an accountant (CPA) or tax advisor who knows your circumstances.
