Crypto Wallets — Types and How to Keep Them Safe

A beginner's guide to what a crypto wallet really holds, how hot and cold wallets and custodial and self-custody setups differ, and which simple habits help protect your keys.

What a Crypto Wallet Really Holds

The word "wallet" is a little misleading. A regular wallet holds notes and coins, but a crypto wallet holds no coins at all. The coins themselves are recorded on the blockchain — a public ledger that tracks which address holds how much. What the wallet stores is the keys that let you prove an address is yours and approve transfers out of it.

Think of a wallet as a keyring rather than a safe. Whoever holds the keys controls the assets, and whoever loses them loses access — even though the coins still appear on the ledger.

Public Key, Private Key and Seed Phrase

Every wallet has two kinds of keys. Your address is derived from the public key — like a bank account number, it can be shared with anyone sending you assets. The private key is the secret: it signs outgoing transfers, and anyone who has it can empty the wallet.

At setup, most wallets show a seed phrase — a list of 12 to 24 ordinary words. They back up all your keys: with them you can restore the wallet on another device if your phone is lost or broken. That is also the danger — anyone who finds the words can do exactly the same.

Hot Wallets vs. Cold Wallets

The distinction is simple: are the keys on an internet-connected device, or not?

  • Hot wallet — a phone app, desktop software or a browser extension. Convenient for regular use, but the constant connection exposes it to malware and hacking attempts.
  • Cold wallet — the keys are kept offline, usually on a dedicated hardware device. Less convenient for frequent activity, but far harder to attack remotely.

No single type suits everyone; each has trade-offs, and some people combine the two.

Who Holds the Keys — You or a Third Party?

This may be the most important question. With a custodial wallet, some organisation — typically a trading venue — holds the keys on your behalf. You log in with a username and password, and a forgotten password can usually be reset. But you depend on that organisation: if it is hacked, collapses or freezes your account, your access may be affected.

With self-custody, the keys are in your hands only. Nobody can freeze your wallet, but nobody can help you if you lose the seed phrase either. The responsibility is entirely yours.

Whoever controls the keys controls the assets. The question is not only which wallet is more convenient, but whom you trust to guard the keys — yourself or someone else.

Basic Safety Habits

Most losses come from simple mistakes, not sophisticated hacks. A few common habits reduce the risk:

  • Write the seed phrase on paper, or another durable physical medium, and keep it somewhere safe — not in a screenshot, an email or cloud storage.
  • Never share the seed phrase with anyone. No legitimate service or support agent will ever ask for it; whoever asks is trying to steal.
  • Watch out for phishing: fake websites, "urgent" messages and links that look almost real. Check the address before typing any details.
  • Before a large transfer, send a small test amount and confirm it arrived. A transfer to a wrong address cannot be reversed.
  • Keep the software up to date and turn on two-factor authentication wherever it is offered.

Risks to Keep in Mind

A good wallet reduces risk; it does not remove it. Human error — a wrong address, a lost backup, a click on a fake link — is a common cause of lost assets, and in crypto there is usually nobody who can reverse it. A custodian is not immune to failure either, and self-custody demands order and discipline over time.

Just as important: keeping your keys safe does not protect you from price swings in the assets themselves. Learn at your own pace, understand each step before taking it, and never act under pressure.
